Choosing a preschool is the first properly consequential education decision you will make, and you will make it with very little information. Every school has a nice website. Every school will tell you they are child-centred. Almost every school in Colombo now calls itself Montessori.

This is the list we would use ourselves. It is organised by what you can check before you visit, what to ask during the visit, and what to notice that nobody will point out to you.

## Before you go: three things to check from your phone

**1. Is the school registered?**
Preschools in Sri Lanka should be registered with the relevant Provincial Council / Ministry of Education authority for early childhood development. Ask for the registration and expect to be shown it without fuss. A school that gets defensive about this question has answered it.

**2. What do the reviews actually say?**
Ignore the star rating; read the text. Five reviews describing specific teachers by name are worth more than fifty generic five-star ratings. Look specifically for what parents say about *communication* — that is the thing that will affect your daily life most.

**3. How far is it, at 7:45 AM on a Monday?**
Not the map distance. The real one. A school twenty minutes further away is a school you will resent within a month, and proximity matters more than parents expect when your child is sick and you need to collect them at 10 AM.

## On the visit: twelve questions worth asking

Ask to visit **while children are there**. A school that will only show you an empty building on a Saturday is showing you furniture, not a school.

### About the adults

**1. What is your adult-to-child ratio, per room?**
This is the single most predictive number. For under-twos, look for something close to 1:4. For 2–5 year olds, 1:8 or better. Be wary of averages across the whole school — ask room by room.

**2. How long have your teachers been here?**
Staff turnover is the metric nobody advertises. If most teachers arrived this year, ask why. Young children attach to specific people, and losing a beloved teacher mid-year is genuinely hard on them.

**3. What training do the teachers have?**
For a Montessori setting, ask which diploma and from which body. "Montessori-trained" with no further detail is not an answer.

### About the day

**4. Can I see the daily routine, hour by hour?**
You are checking for long, unbroken stretches of child-chosen activity. If the day is chopped into fifteen 20-minute blocks, that is a school running a timetable, not following children.

**5. How much time do children spend outdoors, every day?**
There should be a number, and it should be daily, not weather-permitting-in-theory.

**6. What happens when a child does not want to join in?**
The answer tells you the school's whole philosophy. You are listening for curiosity about the child's reason, not a strategy for compliance.

### About safety and honesty

**7. What is your sick-child policy, and how quickly will you call me?**
**8. Who is allowed to collect my child, and how is that checked?**
**9. Show me where the children have their meals and where they wash their hands.**

Ask to see, not to be told. A school proud of its kitchen will walk you there immediately.

### About the difficult conversations

**10. How do you handle biting, hitting, or a child who is having a hard time?**
Any school claiming these things do not happen is either not being honest or not watching closely. You want to hear a considered, non-punitive approach and, crucially, that they will tell you about it.

**11. How and when will you tell me something is wrong?**
Ask for a specific example of when they last raised a concern with a parent. A school that has never had to do this is not observing carefully.

**12. What are your fees, in total, for a year?**
Not the monthly rate. The total, including admission fee, term fees, materials, meals, uniform, events and the annual increase. Get it in writing. (We publish ours openly, and we think every school should.)

## What to notice that nobody will tell you

The tour is a performance. These are the things that are not.

**Look at the children's faces, not the displays.** Are they absorbed? Wandering? Watching the adults for cues? A room of genuinely concentrating children has a distinctive, busy hum — not silence, and not chaos.

**Look at the wall art.** Twenty near-identical crafts means an adult did most of the work. Twenty wildly different ones means the children did. Only one of those is evidence of anything.

**Watch how an adult speaks to a child who is upset** — ideally one who does not know you are watching. This is the most honest thing you will see all day.

**Check whether the materials are within children's reach.** Beautiful equipment on a high shelf is decoration.

**Notice whether anyone gets down to your child's eye level** during the visit. Someone should, and it should look like a habit, not a demonstration.

## The questions to ask yourself afterwards

Sit in the car for five minutes before driving off.

- Could I picture my child here on a bad day, not just a good one?
- Did they answer my hardest question directly, or manage it?
- Did anyone ask me anything about my child?

That last one matters more than people realise. A school that spends the whole visit talking and never asks about your child's temperament, sleep, siblings, or what they love is a school that will be doing the same in six months.

## Trust the boring stuff

The exciting things — the new building, the tablets, the extracurricular list — are the things that will matter least. What will matter is whether the adults are calm, whether they stay, whether they tell you the truth, and whether your child is happy to walk in on a Tuesday morning in the third week of term.
