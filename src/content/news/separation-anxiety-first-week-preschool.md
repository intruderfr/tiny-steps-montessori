Almost every child cries at drop-off at some point. It is not a sign that you chose the wrong school, that your child is unusually fragile, or that you have done something wrong. It is a sign that your child is securely attached to you, which is exactly what you spent the last three years building.

That does not make the car park afterwards any easier. Here is what actually helps.

## Before the first day

**Visit together, more than once.** Familiarity does most of the work. If your school allows a second short visit before the start date, take it.

**Name the teacher constantly.** "Miss Nadeeka will be there." A named adult is far less frightening than "school".

**Do not oversell it.** "It will be SO much fun!" sets up a comparison your child will hold you to at 8:30 AM on Tuesday. Better: "It will feel new. Some bits you will like. I will come back at snack time."

**Practise the separation.** Twenty minutes with a grandparent, and — this is the important half — *come back when you said you would*. You are building evidence that you return.

**Sort out the logistics the night before.** Bag packed, clothes out, water bottle filled. A rushed, tense morning transfers straight onto the child.

## The goodbye itself

This is the part parents get wrong most often, and the fix is small.

**Have a short, identical goodbye ritual.** Two kisses and a wave from the window. The same three words. It should take under thirty seconds, and it should be the same every single day. Predictability is the thing that reduces anxiety — not duration.

**Say goodbye and then actually go.** Lingering extends the hard part and teaches your child that enough distress restarts the negotiation.

**Never sneak out.** It feels kinder in the moment and it is the single most damaging thing you can do. A child who discovers their parent can vanish without warning becomes *more* clingy, because now they cannot relax even when you are in the room.

**Do not let your own face fall.** They are reading you for whether this place is safe. Cry in the car — most of us have.

## The bit nobody warns you about: week three

Week one is often oddly fine. It is new, there is a novelty effect, and the school is on high alert.

Then around week two or three, the novelty wears off, your child works out that this is a *permanent* arrangement, and the crying starts — or restarts, worse than before. Parents frequently panic here, assuming a settled child has regressed.

This is developmentally normal and it is not a signal to withdraw. It usually resolves within two weeks. Tell the school you have hit it; they will have seen it a hundred times.

## What good schools do at drop-off

Worth watching for, because it separates settings quickly:

- Someone is **at the door, expecting your child by name**
- A staff member **takes your child physically**, rather than leaving them to walk in alone while crying
- They offer to **message you in twenty minutes** — and then do
- They tell you the truth: "She cried for ten minutes, then settled" rather than a reflexive "She was fine!"

If you are not getting that, ask for it directly. It is a completely reasonable request.

## How long the crying really lasts

The honest answer from inside the room: **most children stop within five to fifteen minutes of the parent leaving.** They are frequently absorbed in something before the parent has reached the gate.

Ask your school to tell you the actual number of minutes. Not "she was fine" — the number. It is the most reassuring piece of information available to you, and any school observing properly will know it.

## When it is worth a longer conversation

Most separation distress resolves in two to four weeks. Raise it properly with the school if:

- Your child is still distressed **more than 30 minutes** after you leave, **six weeks** in
- The distress is **escalating** rather than easing, week on week
- There are new symptoms at home — sleep disruption, appetite changes, regression in toileting — that persist beyond a few weeks
- Your child says something specific about a person or a situation at school that concerns you

None of these means something is badly wrong. All of them mean the settling-in plan needs adjusting — shorter days for a while, a different key adult, a different arrival time.

## What helps at home

**Keep the day predictable.** Same wake time, same breakfast, same route.

**Do not interrogate at pickup.** "What did you do today?" is an exam question to a three-year-old, and "nothing" is a reasonable answer. Try "What made you laugh?" or, better, say nothing at all for ten minutes and let it come out on its own.

**Expect them to fall apart at home.** Children hold themselves together all morning and then release it at the person they trust most. A meltdown at 4 PM is often evidence the day went well, not badly.

**Protect sleep.** New school is exhausting. Move bedtime earlier for the first month, not later.
