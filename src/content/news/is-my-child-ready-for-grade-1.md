Parents preparing for Grade 1 almost always ask about the same two things: can my child read, and can my child write.

Those are genuinely not the skills that determine how the first year goes. Teachers of young primary classes will tell you the children who struggle are rarely the ones who arrived without letters. They are the ones who cannot yet manage themselves in a group.

## The skills that actually predict a smooth start

### Self-management

- Can go to the toilet independently, including managing their own clothes
- Can open their own lunch box, water bottle and bag
- Can put on and fasten their own shoes
- Can find their own things and put them back

This sounds trivial. It is not. A child who needs an adult for each of these, in a class of thirty-five, spends a large part of their day waiting and feeling incapable.

### Attention and persistence

- Can stay with one task for fifteen to twenty minutes
- Can keep going when something is difficult, at least for a while
- Can follow an instruction with two or three steps

This is the one Montessori children usually arrive strong in, because the long uninterrupted work cycle trains exactly this.

### Social capability

- Can wait for a turn
- Can ask an adult for help, out loud, in a group setting
- Can recover from a disagreement without an adult resolving every one
- Can work near other children without being derailed by them

**Being able to ask for help in front of others** is the one most often missed. A capable child who will not raise their hand can look like a struggling child for an entire term.

### Emotional regulation

- Can manage disappointment without a full collapse
- Can separate from you at the gate
- Can cope with a change of plan

### And yes, some academics

Genuinely useful before Grade 1:

- Recognises and writes their own name
- Knows most letter **sounds** (not names)
- Counts objects accurately to about twenty
- Holds a pencil comfortably
- Can cut along a line with scissors

Notice what is *not* on that list: reading fluently, writing sentences, times tables. Children who arrive already reading do not stay ahead for long; by around age eight the difference has typically evened out.

## What to do in the final year

**Hand over the logistics.** Stop packing their bag. Let them do it, badly, with a picture list. Let them be the one who remembers the water bottle — including the day they forget it.

**Build stamina, not content.** Fifteen minutes of anything they chose, uninterrupted, is worth more than an hour of worksheets. Puzzles, Lego, drawing, digging. The skill is staying, not the subject.

**Practise the shape of school.** Putting a hand up. Waiting to be called on. Finishing something because it is time, not because it is done. In Montessori settings this is the one genuine adjustment, and it is worth doing deliberately in the last term rather than leaving it to September.

**Read to them, every day, well past the point they can read themselves.** This does more for later comprehension than any phonics programme.

**Let them lose.** Board games where you do not let them win. Learning to be disappointed and carry on is a school skill.

**Talk about it in specifics.** "There will be more children." "You will have one teacher for the whole day." "Lunch will be at a set time." Concrete beats reassuring.

## The tempting thing that backfires

Every year some families put a five-year-old into extra tuition to get a head start.

The measurable academic advantage is small and short-lived. The cost — a child who has decided by age six that learning is a chore imposed by adults — is neither small nor short-lived. Curiosity is the single most valuable thing your child brings to Grade 1, and it is much easier to extinguish than to rebuild.

## If your child is not ready

Some children genuinely need another term or year, most often because they are young for the intake, or because they need more time on emotional regulation.

This is not a failure and it does not follow them. Being the oldest and most capable in a class is a considerably better experience than being the youngest and most stretched. Talk to both the preschool and the primary school; both will have seen it many times.

## Two things worth telling the new school

**Whatever is going on at home.** New baby, bereavement, a parent working abroad, a move. Teachers can only account for what they know.

**How your child asks for help — or does not.** If your child goes quiet rather than asking, say so on day one. It is the single most useful sentence a Grade 1 teacher can hear in September.
