We run a Montessori school, so you should read this knowing where we stand. What follows is still as fair as we can make it, because a child placed in the wrong setting is not good for anyone — including us.

## The core difference in one sentence

In a traditional preschool, the adult decides what the class does next. In a Montessori classroom, the child decides what they do next, from a carefully prepared set of options.

Nearly every other difference falls out of that one.

## Side by side

| | Montessori | Traditional |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who chooses the activity** | The child, from prepared materials | The teacher, for the group |
| **Class grouping** | Mixed ages, usually a 3-year span | Same age, one year band |
| **Work period** | One long uninterrupted block, up to 3 hours | Short rotating blocks, 15–30 min |
| **Teacher's role** | Observes; gives individual lessons | Leads the group from the front |
| **Materials** | Specific, self-correcting, one of each | Varied, plentiful, often themed |
| **Pace** | Each child's own | The group's |
| **Assessment** | Written observation | Often grades, stars, charts |
| **Motivation** | The satisfaction of the work | Praise, stickers, rewards |

## What Montessori does genuinely well

**Concentration.** The long uninterrupted work cycle is the whole point. A child allowed to stay with something for forty minutes develops an attention span that a 20-minute rotation actively prevents.

**Independence.** Everything is child-height and child-sized. Children pour their own drinks, clean their own spills, fetch and return their own work. Parents usually notice this at home within the first term, and it is often the change they comment on first.

**Self-correcting materials.** The knobbed cylinders only fit one way. The child discovers their own error without an adult marking it. Over time this builds a child who checks their own work rather than looking up for approval.

**Mixed ages.** Younger children see where they are going; older children consolidate by teaching. The social dynamic is noticeably gentler than a room of twenty same-age three-year-olds.

## What traditional preschool does genuinely well

We would be dishonest to skip this.

**Structure suits some children.** A child who finds open choice overwhelming can flourish with a clear, predictable, adult-led sequence. Freedom is not automatically a gift.

**Group experience.** Circle time, whole-class singing, group performances and a class play build a shared identity that a Montessori room, with everyone doing something different, generates less naturally.

**Direct preparation for a conventional Grade 1.** Sri Lankan primary school is mostly adult-directed, seated and group-paced. A traditional preschool rehearses that daily.

**Simplicity.** Traditional practice is more uniform. Montessori quality varies enormously between schools — which brings us to the real problem.

## The real problem: "Montessori" means very little on a signboard

The name is not trademarked. Any preschool in Sri Lanka can use it. Many do, on the strength of owning a pink tower.

Three checks that cut through it, all of which you can do on a visit:

1. **Is there a genuine three-hour uninterrupted work cycle?** Ask to see the timetable. If the morning is chopped into short adult-led blocks, it is not Montessori practice regardless of the materials.
2. **Are the ages mixed?** A 2–5 classroom is Montessori. Separate rooms for each year is a traditional structure with Montessori equipment.
3. **What training do the guides have — which diploma, from which body?** Expect a specific answer.

A traditional preschool that is honest about being traditional is a much better choice than a school calling itself Montessori and running neither.

## Which children do better where

This is the part most comparisons avoid.

**Montessori tends to suit** children who get absorbed in things and resent being interrupted; children who want to do it themselves; quieter children who find large-group instruction hard; and children with uneven development — racing ahead in one area, taking their time in another — because they are not held to a group pace.

**Traditional tends to suit** children who thrive on routine and clear expectations; deeply social children who love group activity; children who find too much choice paralysing; and families who want the structure to closely resemble what comes next.

**Either works well** for the majority of children. Most children are adaptable, and the specific adults in the room will matter more than the philosophy on the gate.

## What actually predicts a good outcome

If we had to reduce it to four things, none of them is the method:

1. **The ratio.** How many adults, per room, per child.
2. **Staff turnover.** Whether the person your child attaches to is still there in a year.
3. **Whether the adults are warm.** Watch how someone handles an upset child.
4. **Whether the school tells you the truth** when something is not going well.

A warm, well-staffed traditional preschool will beat a cold, understaffed Montessori school every single time.

## And afterwards?

The common worry is that a Montessori child will struggle in a conventional Grade 1 — that after years of choosing their own work, sitting still through a directed lesson will be a shock.

In our experience the transition is fine, and the reason is concentration. A child who has held their attention on a chosen task for forty minutes at a stretch has the underlying capacity to attend to a lesson. What genuinely needs practising is the *form*: putting your hand up, working to someone else's timing, doing something because you were asked to. That is a matter of weeks, and it is worth telling your child's final preschool year that Grade 1 is coming so they can build it in deliberately.
