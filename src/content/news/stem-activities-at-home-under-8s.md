Most "STEM kits" are a craft in a box with a predetermined outcome. That is not science. Science is having a question you do not know the answer to.

Every activity below uses things already in your house, and every one has a genuine unknown. The **question to ask** matters more than the activity — it is what turns a fun mess into thinking.

## Science

### 1. The sink-or-float tray
A basin of water and fifteen random objects. Predict first, out loud, one by one. Then test.
**Ask:** *"This one is heavier than that one but it floated. Why do you think that is?"*
**Concept:** density — it is not about heavy or light, it is about heavy *for its size*.

### 2. Red cabbage indicator
Boil chopped red cabbage, keep the purple water. Add a splash to vinegar, lemon juice, baking soda solution, soap.
**Ask:** *"Can you sort these into groups by what colour they turned?"*
**Concept:** acids and bases. Pink for acid, green-blue for alkaline.

### 3. Skittles or sugar-syrup layers
Coloured sugar water at different concentrations, poured slowly down the back of a spoon.
**Ask:** *"Why did the heaviest one go to the bottom instead of mixing?"*
**Concept:** density again, now visible in a single glass.

### 4. Grow the same seed four ways
Four mung bean sprouts: one in the dark, one with no water, one in the fridge, one on the windowsill. Measure daily on the same chart.
**Ask:** *"Which one would you bet on tomorrow? Write it down."*
**Concept:** controlled variables — changing one thing at a time.

### 5. Melting race
Ice cubes on a plate, on a metal spoon, wrapped in cloth, in a bowl of water.
**Ask:** *"The metal feels colder. So why did that one melt fastest?"*
**Concept:** conduction. The counterintuitive result is the whole point.

## Engineering

### 6. Paper bridge, tested to destruction
One A4 sheet across a 20cm gap between two books. Load it with coins until it fails. Then fold, corrugate or roll the paper and try again.
**Ask:** *"Same paper, ten times stronger. What did the folding actually do?"*
**Concept:** structural geometry. Shape carries load, not material.

### 7. The spaghetti tower
Twenty pieces of dry spaghetti, a metre of tape, and a marshmallow that must sit on top. Twenty minutes.
**Ask:** *"Build a small one that works first, then make it taller."*
**Concept:** iterative design — and, quietly, that a triangle does not deform.

### 8. Balloon car
A balloon, a straw, tape, and a base on wheels (bottle caps and skewers, or a toy car).
**Ask:** *"How do we make it go further? Change one thing at a time."*
**Concept:** thrust, friction and fair testing.

### 9. Syringe hydraulics
Two syringes joined by a length of aquarium tubing, filled with water. Push one, the other moves. Mount them to lift a cardboard arm.
**Ask:** *"Why does water push back but air just squashes?"*
**Concept:** hydraulics — liquids do not compress.

## Technology and Mathematics

### 10. Robot parent
Your child gives you instructions to make a sandwich. You follow them with absolute literalism. "Put the jam on the bread" gets the closed jar placed on the loaf.
**Ask:** *"That is not what you meant. So what exactly should you have said?"*
**Concept:** unplugged coding — precision, sequence and debugging. This is the single best programming activity for a six-year-old, and it needs no computer.

### 11. Measure everything
A tape measure and a notebook. Every doorway, every family member, the cat if it consents. Then order the results.
**Ask:** *"Estimate first. How close were you?"*
**Concept:** estimation and units — estimation is a trainable skill and it is what makes later arithmetic errors visible.

### 12. Symmetry hunt
A small mirror and a walk around the house or garden. Find things with a line of symmetry — leaves, tiles, faces, letters.
**Ask:** *"Is a leaf really symmetrical, or nearly?"*
**Concept:** symmetry, and the difference between mathematical ideals and the real world.

## Four rules that matter more than the activities

**1. Let them be wrong first.** Predictions before testing, every time. A disproved prediction is worth ten correct ones, because it is the moment learning actually happens.

**2. Do not rescue the build.** When the tower falls and you take over, the lesson becomes "an adult fixes this". Sit on your hands.

**3. "I do not know — how could we find out?"** is the best sentence in your repertoire. You are not supposed to know the answers; you are supposed to model looking.

**4. Write it down.** Even a scrawled prediction and a number. Recording turns messing about into an experiment, and children take it noticeably more seriously.

## What to skip

- Kits with one predetermined outcome and step-by-step instructions
- Anything where the adult does the interesting part
- Apps that call themselves STEM but are a quiz with animations
- Explaining the concept before they have played with it — always hands first, words second
