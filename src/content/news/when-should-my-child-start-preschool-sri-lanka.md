This question arrives in our inbox more than any other, usually from a parent whose child is around two, and usually with an anxious edge — a cousin's child already started, a neighbour said three is too late, someone mentioned a waiting list.

Here is the honest answer: the age matters far less than you have been led to believe, and the readiness signs are not the ones most people list.

## What the typical Sri Lankan timeline looks like

Most preschools in the Colombo area work to roughly this shape:

| Age | Typical setting |
| --- | --- |
| 1 – 2 years | Playgroup, short sessions, parent settling-in |
| 2 – 3 years | First full preschool year |
| 3 – 5 years | Main preschool / Montessori years |
| 5+ years | Transition to Grade 1 |

Since children generally enter Grade 1 at around five, most families want two to three years of preschool before that. Working backwards, that puts a common start somewhere between two and three.

But that is a description of what people do, not a prescription of what your child needs.

## The readiness signs that actually matter

Notice that none of these are academic.

**They can be comforted by someone who is not you.** Not that they never get upset — that when they do, another trusted adult can settle them. This is the strongest single predictor of a smooth start.

**They show interest in other children.** Watching, following, hovering at the edge of a group. They do not need to play *with* other children — parallel play is completely age-appropriate and real cooperative play typically arrives closer to four.

**They can manage a short separation.** Ten minutes with a grandparent while you step out. If this is already fine, a preschool morning will be too.

**They have some way to communicate a need.** Words, signs, pointing, pulling you by the hand. It does not have to be speech.

**They can stay awake and content through a morning.** If your child still sleeps at 9:30 AM, a morning session will be a battle. Wait a few months.

## The signs that do not matter

Parents worry about these constantly, and none of them should delay a start:

- **Not toilet trained.** Any good setting for under-threes handles nappies as routine. If a school makes toilet training a condition of entry, that tells you about the school.
- **Not talking much yet.** Preschool very often accelerates language, because there is suddenly a reason to use it.
- **Very shy.** Shy children usually do well, given a slow settling-in. Being pushed in fast is the thing that harms them, not starting itself.
- **Still breastfeeding, still using a dummy, still co-sleeping.** None of this is anyone's business but yours.

## Is it a problem to start at four?

No. Genuinely, no.

Children who start at four typically settle *faster* than children who start at two, because they arrive with more language, more independence and more capacity to manage separation. They tend to close any gap within a term.

The one thing worth planning for: if your child will only have a year before Grade 1, tell the school that at the start. A good school will make sure the transition work — sitting for a longer stretch, managing their own things, being part of a bigger group — happens in that year rather than being left to the last few weeks.

## Is it a problem to start at eighteen months?

Also no, provided the setting is genuinely built for that age. What "built for that age" means in practice:

- A ratio near **1 adult to 4 children**
- **Short sessions** — two to three hours, not a full day
- **Unhurried settling-in**, over weeks, with you present at first
- Adults who see nappies, comfort objects and crying as ordinary parts of the day

An eighteen-month-old in a room designed for three-year-olds will not thrive, regardless of how lovely the school is.

## The one factor most families underweight

Your own readiness.

If you are going back to work, if you are managing a new baby, if you are exhausted — those are legitimate reasons to start, and you do not need to dress them up as being about the child. A parent who has some support is a better parent, and children pick that up.

Equally, if you have the option to keep your child home another six months and you want to, that is fine too. There is no developmental cliff you are missing.

## What we would actually suggest

1. **Visit two or three schools while children are there.** You will learn more in twenty minutes of watching than from any amount of reading.
2. **Ask what settling-in looks like, in days.** The answer separates schools that understand young children from schools that do not.
3. **Start at the beginning of a term if you can.** Joining alongside other new children is easier than joining a settled group.
4. **Give it six weeks before judging.** Weeks two and three are frequently harder than week one. That is normal and it passes.
