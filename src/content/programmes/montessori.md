## What actually makes a classroom Montessori

The word gets used loosely in Colombo. Plenty of preschools own a pink tower and call themselves Montessori. What matters is not the furniture — it is whether the child is genuinely free to choose their work, and whether they get long enough to go deep into it.

Two things distinguish a real Montessori environment, and you can check both on a school visit:

1. **The three-hour uninterrupted work cycle.** Children choose their own activity and stay with it for as long as it holds them. No bell, no whole-class instruction, no being moved on just as they were getting somewhere.
2. **The mixed-age classroom.** Our 2–5 classroom holds all three years together. The four-year-old teaching a two-year-old how to carry a tray learns more from that moment than any worksheet could give them.

Ask any school you visit whether they do both. The answer tells you almost everything.

## The five areas of the prepared environment

### Practical Life
Pouring, sweeping, buttoning, polishing, cutting fruit. This looks like housework and it is the most important shelf in the room. It builds the concentration, sequencing and hand control that reading and writing later sit on top of.

### Sensorial
Materials that isolate one quality at a time — the pink tower for size, the colour tablets for hue, the sound cylinders for pitch. The child's job is to classify the world, and these give them the categories to do it with.

### Language
Sandpaper letters traced with two fingers, the moveable alphabet, then reading. Because letters are learned by sound and by touch, most of our children write before they read — often before their fifth birthday, and without being drilled.

### Mathematics
Number rods, spindle boxes, the golden beads. Abstract mathematics is introduced as something you can hold in your hands first. A child who has physically carried a thousand-cube understands place value in a way a memorised table never delivers.

### Culture
Geography, botany, zoology, art and music. Sri Lanka on the map, then the continents, then the solar system — always concrete first, always something to handle.

## Where the guide fits

A Montessori guide does not stand at the front. They observe, they give individual lessons at the moment a particular child is ready for a particular material, and then they get out of the way.

That is why the ratio matters here too: **one guide to eight children**. It is not crowd control; it is having enough attention available to notice that a specific child is finally ready for the moveable alphabet today.

## How we report progress

You will not receive a grade. Marks are meaningless at this age and encourage exactly the comparison Montessori is designed to avoid.

Instead you get a written observation each term describing what your child has been drawn to, which materials they have mastered, how they are getting on socially, and what we expect to come next. Plus a parent meeting to talk it through properly.

> A child who has been allowed to concentrate deeply for three hours is calm, capable and unusually kind to the people around them. That is the real outcome, and you will notice it at home long before you notice it on paper.
